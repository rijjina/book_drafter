# Generalized Thai Teaching-Writing Style

Use this reference when drafting a different subject from the one represented by legacy
teaching documents, or when the user asks to preserve the author's teaching voice
without reusing old course content.

## Evidence boundary

This profile was distilled from a legacy Thai teaching-material set containing a course
plan, front matter, and ten chapter-level DOCX files. The analysis retained only
structural and stylistic measurements. It did not retain course claims, specialist
terms, examples, citations, names, course codes, or learning outcomes.

Treat the profile as a descriptive baseline, not a governing standard. A supplied
institutional template, the new discipline's conventions, accessibility, accuracy, and
citation integrity take precedence.

## Authorial stance

- Use formal, explanatory Thai directed to students who must be able to read
  independently outside class.
- Keep the body predominantly impersonal. Reserve first-person language for a preface,
  a clearly identified reflection, or genuine instructor experience that the author has
  supplied.
- Explain relationships and consequences rather than presenting compressed labels or
  slide bullets as finished prose.
- Maintain a calm instructional tone: authoritative enough to guide, but not inflated,
  promotional, or self-congratulatory.

## Paragraph rhythm

- Use moderate paragraphs, normally one main idea developed through two to five
  sentences. Split a paragraph when it shifts from definition to mechanism, mechanism
  to example, or example to application.
- Begin with a topic sentence or a direct definition, then add conditions,
  relationships, consequences, or limitations.
- Use lists for genuine sequences, criteria, components, or procedures. Do not convert
  connected explanation into fragments merely to shorten the page.
- Use parentheses selectively for the English term, abbreviation, date, citation, or a
  concise qualification; move substantial explanation into prose.

## Explanatory sequence

The recurring transferable sequence is:

1. orient the learner to the topic and its relevance;
2. state learning objectives or expected outcomes when supported;
3. define the central concept and establish terminology;
4. explain components, relationships, mechanisms, or steps;
5. introduce a verified example, case, comparison, or worked application;
6. connect the concept to a learning activity or professional use where appropriate;
7. synthesize the section or chapter;
8. ask aligned questions or provide exercises;
9. list references and source/rights notes.

This is a routing pattern, not a mandatory heading list. Combine or omit components when
the document type, subject, chapter purpose, or institutional format requires it.

## Language and terminology

- Write the Thai term first when a stable Thai term exists; give the English term in
  parentheses at first meaningful use. Introduce an abbreviation once, then use it
  consistently.
- Keep technical English when translation would reduce precision, but explain its role
  in Thai rather than accumulating unexplained foreign terms.
- Prefer direct connectors that express the real relationship: addition, contrast,
  condition, cause and effect, sequence, or conclusion. Do not add ornamental
  transitions mechanically.
- Distinguish direct evidence, explanation, interpretation, and proposed application.

## Examples, activities, and questions

- Examples are frequent teaching devices, not decoration. Each example should clarify
  the concept immediately before it and should come from a verified source or a clearly
  labeled constructed scenario.
- Follow a worked example with an explanation of why the result matters or which
  principle it demonstrates.
- Design activities and questions from the stated learning objective and the content
  actually taught. Avoid questions that test material absent from the chapter.
- Mix recall, explanation, application, comparison, and analysis in proportion to the
  course level. Do not copy legacy questions into another discipline.

## Tables and visual structure

- Use tables for comparisons, repeated records, criteria, procedures, or compact
  mappings. Use prose for reasoning and interpretation.
- Introduce every table or figure in the body, give it a functional caption, and explain
  the point the learner should take from it.
- Preserve the legacy preference for readable Thai typography only when compatible with
  the current institution. A common baseline is TH Sarabun New with a clear body/heading
  hierarchy, but the governing template overrides font and size.
- Use native heading styles, automatic numbering where appropriate, and a consistent
  caption system. Do not reproduce legacy manual-heading inconsistencies.

## Cross-course conversion checklist

Before delivery, confirm that:

- no old subject term, claim, example, citation, course identifier, person, or outcome
  remains merely because it appeared in the style reference;
- every definition and factual claim is supported by the new course's sources;
- examples and activities fit the new learners and discipline;
- terminology and abbreviations are consistent within the new document;
- the chapter closes with a synthesis and assessments aligned to its actual objectives;
- the DOCX has been rendered and visually inspected.
